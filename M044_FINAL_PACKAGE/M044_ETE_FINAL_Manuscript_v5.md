# Abstract

**Background.** The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) eighth edition removed microscopic extrathyroidal extension (ETE) from T3 upstaging while retaining gross ETE as an adverse anatomic feature. Whether gross ETE confers higher recurrence risk than microscopic ETE in surgical cohorts remains debated.

**Methods.** We performed a single-institution retrospective cohort study of a locked, strict differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) cohort. ETE was classified as no/negative, microscopic, or gross from pathology-derived fields. The primary endpoint was path-proven structural recurrence. Multivariable logistic regression used microscopic ETE as the reference and adjusted for age, sex, log-transformed tumor size, AJCC eighth-edition N stage, five-level histology, and separate five-level lymphatic and vascular invasion categories. Prespecified sensitivity models added radioactive iodine (RAI) and BRAF/TERT status; exploratory models added lymph-node topography, restricted the cohort to papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), substituted a composite lymphovascular-invasion (LVI) flag, and excluded patients with zero recorded follow-up.

**Results.** The cohort included 3,578 patients (no/negative ETE, n = 68; microscopic ETE, n = 2,359; gross ETE, n = 1,151), with 105 path-proven recurrences. Crude path-proven recurrence rates were 4.4 %, 1.9 %, and 5.0 %, respectively. In the primary model (N = 3,572; 105 events), gross ETE was associated with higher recurrence odds than microscopic ETE (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.77; 95 % confidence interval [CI], 1.15–2.71; *p* = 0.009). The gross-vs-microscopic association was directionally consistent across crude (OR 2.68), BRAF/TERT-adjusted (1.78), lymph-node-topography (1.99), PTC-only (1.97), composite-LVI (2.04), and zero-follow-up–excluded (1.79) specifications, but attenuated when RAI was added (1.40), consistent with confounding by indication. The no/negative-vs-microscopic contrast was imprecise (aOR 2.72; 95 % CI, 0.80–9.30) and confounded by follow-up, repeat surgery, margin, RAI, and referral-pattern differences.

**Conclusions.** Gross, but not microscopic, ETE showed a reproducible adjusted association with path-proven recurrence, supporting the AJCC eighth-edition separation of microscopic from gross ETE while preserving microscopic ETE as a contextual feature in individualized recurrence-risk assessment.

# Introduction

Extrathyroidal extension (ETE) has long served as a marker of anatomic extent and biologic aggressiveness in differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC). The eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system made a major conceptual change by removing microscopic ETE from T3 upstaging while retaining gross ETE as a staging-relevant adverse feature, including separate recognition of gross strap-muscle invasion (T3b) and more extensive invasion into aerodigestive, neural, or vascular structures (T4a/T4b).¹,² This revision aligned AJCC staging with its principal purpose — prediction of disease-specific mortality — and reflected growing evidence that microscopic ETE is not biologically or prognostically equivalent to gross invasion.

The recurrence implications of microscopic ETE remain less settled. Some studies have linked microscopic ETE to nodal metastasis, lymphovascular invasion (LVI), positive margins, and recurrence, whereas others suggest microscopic ETE is not independently prognostic after adjustment for tumor size, nodal disease, histology, and other adverse features.⁴⁻⁸ These discrepancies likely reflect heterogeneity in pathology definitions, analytic reference groups, treatment selection, follow-up intensity, endpoint adjudication, and cohort composition.

Pathology remains the reference standard for microscopic ETE, whereas preoperative imaging is more clinically useful for suspected gross invasion. Cross-sectional imaging can inform surgical planning when gross extension is present, but negative imaging cannot exclude minimal perithyroidal soft-tissue involvement. This distinction matters clinically, because staging, operative planning, adjuvant therapy, and surveillance decisions require integration of pathologic ETE grade with nodal burden, tumor size, LVI, histology, margin status, molecular markers when available, postoperative thyroglobulin, and dynamic response to therapy.³,⁹

We evaluated a locked, strict-DTC surgical cohort to test whether gross ETE confers higher recurrence risk than microscopic ETE after adjustment for key clinicopathologic covariates. We additionally and explicitly audited the small no/negative ETE subgroup, because in a retrospective academic surgical cohort this group was not expected to function as a clean biologic comparator. To our knowledge, this is the largest single-institution strict-DTC surgical cohort analysis to focus specifically on path-proven recurrence with an explicit no/negative ETE audit and a prespecified gross-vs-microscopic ETE contrast.

# Materials and Methods

## Study design and cohort

We performed a single-institution retrospective cohort study using the institutional thyroid cancer publication database, specifically `manuscript_workspace.cohort_m044_ajcc_ete_v1`. The parent M044 cohort included 4,128 patients. Strict DTC was defined a priori; cases were excluded for non-DTC histology (medullary thyroid carcinoma, anaplastic carcinoma, follicular tumor of uncertain malignant potential [FTUMP], non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features [NIFTP], follicular adenoma and atypical follicular/Hurthle-cell neoplasms, NUT carcinoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma, thymic-like carcinoma, and high-grade carcinoma with focal squamous features), metastatic-only or recurrent-only presentations, and ETE grade missing, ungraded, or unclassifiable into the three primary groups. After 550 sequential exclusions, the locked analytic cohort comprised 3,578 patients. The cohort lock was dated 2026-05-04. Cohort assembly is shown in Figure 1. Institutional review board status, waiver of informed consent, and data-use approvals are stated in the Declarations.

## Exposure and endpoints

The primary exposure was the pathology-derived ETE group: no/negative ETE, microscopic ETE, or gross ETE. Microscopic ETE served as the reference category in all regression models. The prespecified primary endpoint was path-proven structural recurrence, defined by biopsy, cytology, or operative pathology confirmation (`recurrence_path_proven`, n = 105). Imaging-only unconfirmed recurrence (n = 136) and a composite endpoint of path-proven or imaging-only recurrence (n = 241) were prespecified secondary/descriptive endpoints. Two legacy any-recurrence flags inconsistent with the canonical resolved-recurrence schema were retained for audit-only transparency and were not used as primary or secondary endpoints.

## Covariates

The primary multivariable model adjusted for age at surgery (per 10 years), sex (male reference), ln(1 + tumor size in cm), AJCC eighth-edition N stage (N0 reference; N1a, N1b, Nx), five-level histology (PTC reference; FTC, poorly-differentiated DTC, high-grade DTC; metastatic-PTC dummy structurally zero in cohort and dropped), five-level lymphatic invasion (missing reference; present, extensive, focal, indeterminate), and five-level vascular invasion (missing reference; present-ungraded, extensive, focal, indeterminate). Lymphatic and vascular invasion were modeled separately rather than as a single composite LVI flag. RAI was excluded from the primary model because receipt is strongly confounded by indication; RAI, BRAF V600E, and TERT promoter status were added in prespecified sensitivity models.

## Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR) or n (%). Continuous variables were compared across the three ETE groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test; categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test on the 3 × k contingency table. The primary analysis used multivariable logistic regression for the binary outcome of observed path-proven recurrence. Binary logistic regression was chosen a priori because follow-up was short and irregular, with 1,187 patients (33.2 %) having zero recorded follow-up years. Odds ratios (ORs), 95 % confidence intervals (CIs), and two-sided *p* values are reported. Quasi-separated dummies were detected automatically and either dropped or flagged as not estimable. Two-sided α = 0.05.

## Sensitivity and exploratory analyses

Two prespecified sensitivity models added (1) RAI receipt and (2) BRAF V600E and TERT promoter status. Exploratory models additionally added (a) central- and lateral-compartment lymph-node positivity, (b) tested an ETE × ln(1 + lymph-node positive) interaction, (c) restricted the cohort to PTC, (d) substituted a composite "any LVI" flag for the separate five-level lymphatic and vascular invasion variables, and (e) excluded patients with zero recorded follow-up. A stricter sensitivity excluded patients with less than one year of recorded follow-up. Preoperative CT ETE variables were not available in the analytic dataset.

## Explicit no/negative ETE audit

Because the no/negative ETE subgroup was small (n = 68) and showed atypical clinical features, we performed a structured confounding audit comparing composite-recurrent and non-recurrent patients within that subgroup. The audit evaluated demographics, tumor size, multifocality, margin status, N stage, central and lateral lymph-node positivity, total thyroidectomy, RAI receipt, repeat surgery, time from first to second surgery, AGES score, follow-up duration, and recurrence detail. All computations were performed in Python with statsmodels logistic regression and scipy statistical functions; analyses were reproducible from the locked patient-level dataset.

# Results

## Cohort characteristics, demographics, and baseline pathology

The strict-DTC cohort comprised 3,578 patients distributed across three ETE strata: no/negative ETE, n = 68 (1.9 %); microscopic ETE, n = 2,359 (65.9 %); and gross ETE, n = 1,151 (32.2 %) (Table 1). Median age at surgery was 50 years overall (IQR, 38–63) and was similar across groups (no/negative 50 [36.8–58.0], microscopic 50 [38–62], gross 51 [39–64]; *p* = 0.146). The cohort was 74.2 % female, with the gross-ETE group containing a smaller female proportion (69.6 %) than the microscopic-ETE (76.4 %) or no/negative-ETE (75.0 %) groups (*p* < 0.001). Race distribution was approximately 58.6 % white, 24.6 % Black/African American, 6.8 % Asian, and 10.0 % other or unknown, with no significant differences across ETE groups (race-specific *p* values 0.347–0.740). Median body mass index was 28.5 kg/m² (IQR, 24.4–34.0) and did not differ significantly by ETE group (*p* = 0.427).

Tumor characteristics differed substantially across ETE strata. Median greatest tumor dimension was 1.2 cm (IQR, 0.5–2.5) in the no/negative-ETE group, 1.5 cm (0.7–2.6) in the microscopic-ETE group, and 2.1 cm (1.1–4.0) in the gross-ETE group (*p* < 0.001). Multifocal disease (39.4 % vs 34.5 % vs 20.6 %; *p* < 0.001) and bilateral disease (28.0 % vs 20.7 % vs 7.4 %; *p* < 0.001) were more frequent in gross-ETE than microscopic-ETE patients. Margin involvement was substantially more common in gross-ETE (26.2 %) than in microscopic-ETE (8.1 %) but was also elevated in no/negative-ETE (22.1 %; *p* < 0.001), an audit-relevant pattern. Aggressive PTC variant histology was uncommon (1.2 % overall) but was enriched in the gross-ETE group (2.2 % vs 0.7 % microscopic, *p* < 0.001).

AJCC eighth-edition T-stage distribution mirrored the ETE definitions, with all gross-ETE patients classified as T3b and no T4a or T4b cases in the cohort. N-stage distribution was N0 30.9 %, N1a 62.3 %, N1b 1.7 %, and Nx 5.0 %; gross ETE was associated with more N1a (69.3 %) and N1b (3.5 %) and less Nx (1.7 %) than the microscopic-ETE group (*p* < 0.001). AJCC overall stage groups were predominantly Stage I (37.6 %) and Stage II (43.1 %), with very few Stage III or IVA cases; gross ETE was concentrated in Stages II and IVB.

Histology was dominated by PTC (85.3 %), followed by FTC (13.4 %), poorly-differentiated DTC (1.0 %), and high-grade DTC (0.3 %). Lymphatic and vascular invasion patterns varied by ETE group: any vascular invasion was present in 38.5 % of gross-ETE patients versus 22.3 % of microscopic-ETE patients (*p* < 0.001). Central-compartment lymph-node positivity (31.3 % vs 16.9 %), lateral-compartment positivity (14.6 % vs 5.6 %), and the composite any-LVI flag (43.4 % vs 25.2 %) were each more frequent in gross-ETE patients (all *p* < 0.001). Median AGES score increased monotonically across groups (no/negative 6.05; microscopic 7.10; gross 8.30; *p* < 0.001).

Treatment was more aggressive in gross-ETE patients: 75.4 % underwent total thyroidectomy (vs 67.6 % microscopic and 57.4 % no/negative; *p* < 0.001), and 26.6 % received radioactive iodine (vs 11.1 % microscopic and 19.1 % no/negative; *p* < 0.001). The repeat-surgery rate was higher in the no/negative-ETE group (26.5 %) than in the microscopic-ETE (14.9 %) or gross-ETE (14.7 %) groups (*p* = 0.029). BRAF V600E was identified in 7.2 %, TERT promoter mutation in 1.5 %, RAS in 4.8 %, and RET fusion in 0.4 % of patients overall, with no statistically significant differences across ETE groups; molecular testing was not universal.

## Follow-up and recurrence

Median follow-up was 1.12 years overall (IQR, 0.00–4.95). One-third of the cohort (1,187 patients; 33.2 %) had zero recorded follow-up years. Follow-up differed by ETE group: 1.42 years (IQR, 0.00–6.36) in no/negative ETE, 0.70 years (IQR, 0.00–4.34) in microscopic ETE, and 1.99 years (IQR, 0.04–5.65) in gross ETE; the proportion with zero follow-up was 36.8 %, 37.9 %, and 23.4 %, respectively. Path-proven recurrence occurred in 105 patients (2.9 %): 3 with no/negative ETE (4.4 %), 45 with microscopic ETE (1.9 %), and 57 with gross ETE (5.0 %) (Figure 2). Composite recurrence (path-proven or imaging-only unconfirmed) occurred in 241 patients (6.7 %): 4 no/negative-ETE (5.9 %), 116 microscopic-ETE (4.9 %), and 121 gross-ETE patients (10.5 %). Imaging-then-path confirmed recurrence — the subset of path-proven events first identified on imaging — occurred in 21 patients (0.6 %).

## Lymph-node and lymphovascular-invasion patterns

Gross ETE displayed a higher lymphatic-burden phenotype than microscopic ETE. Central-compartment lymph-node positivity was present in 31.3 % of gross-ETE patients versus 16.9 % of microscopic-ETE patients, and lateral-compartment positivity was present in 14.6 % versus 5.6 %, respectively. The composite any-LVI flag was present in 43.4 % of gross-ETE versus 25.2 % of microscopic-ETE patients. Any vascular invasion was also more frequent in gross than microscopic ETE (38.5 % vs 22.3 %).

## Primary multivariable regression

In the primary multivariable model (N = 3,572; 105 events), gross ETE was associated with higher odds of path-proven recurrence than microscopic ETE (aOR, 1.77; 95 % CI, 1.15–2.71; *p* = 0.009; Table 2). The no/negative-vs-microscopic contrast was point-elevated but imprecise (aOR, 2.72; 95 % CI, 0.80–9.30; *p* = 0.111). Tumor size remained independently associated with recurrence (aOR for ln[1 + size], 1.93; 95 % CI, 1.27–2.92; *p* = 0.002). FTC was associated with lower odds of recurrence than PTC (aOR, 0.31; 95 % CI, 0.14–0.71; *p* = 0.006). Focal vascular invasion was independently associated with recurrence (aOR, 2.25; 95 % CI, 1.07–4.73; *p* = 0.033), whereas lymphatic invasion present versus missing was not (aOR, 0.87; 95 % CI, 0.47–1.61; *p* = 0.657). N1b versus N0 trended elevated but did not reach significance (aOR, 2.24; 95 % CI, 0.83–6.03; *p* = 0.110). Nx, high-grade DTC, and lymphatic-focal cells were quasi-separated and were transparently dropped.

## Sensitivity and exploratory models

The gross-vs-microscopic ETE association was directionally consistent across model specifications (Table 3; Figure 3). The crude OR was 2.68 (95 % CI, 1.80–3.99; *p* < 0.001). The BRAF/TERT-adjusted aOR was 1.78 (95 % CI, 1.16–2.73; *p* = 0.008). The lymph-node-topography exploratory aOR was 1.99 (95 % CI, 1.31–3.02; *p* = 0.001), with both central LN+ (aOR 1.78; 95 % CI, 1.04–3.06; *p* = 0.036) and lateral LN+ (aOR 1.85; 95 % CI, 1.05–3.24; *p* = 0.033) entering independently. The ETE × ln(1 + lymph-node positive) interaction was not significant (interaction OR, 0.87; 95 % CI, 0.59–1.28; *p* = 0.485). In the PTC-only model, the aOR was 1.97 (95 % CI, 1.26–3.07; *p* = 0.003); in the composite-LVI model, 2.04 (95 % CI, 1.35–3.09; *p* < 0.001).

Adding RAI attenuated the gross-vs-microscopic estimate to aOR 1.40 (95 % CI, 0.90–2.16; *p* = 0.136), with RAI itself entering the model at aOR 3.72 (95 % CI, 2.45–5.64; *p* < 0.001), consistent with confounding by indication.

In a prespecified sensitivity analysis excluding the 1,187 patients with zero recorded follow-up, the gross-vs-microscopic aOR was 1.79 (95 % CI, 1.17–2.72; *p* = 0.007). With a more restrictive exclusion requiring follow-up of at least one year, the aOR strengthened to 2.10 (95 % CI, 1.30–3.39; *p* = 0.002), and N1b versus N0 reached significance (aOR, 3.88; 95 % CI, 1.34–11.25; *p* = 0.013).

## No/negative ETE confounding audit

The no/negative ETE subgroup contained 68 patients with 4 composite recurrences and 3 path-proven recurrences. Among the 4 composite-recurrent no/negative patients, median follow-up was 9.68 years (IQR, 4.53–13.84) compared with 1.20 years (IQR, 0.00–5.91) among the 64 non-recurrent no/negative patients — an approximately 8-fold difference (Table 4). All 4 recurrent patients underwent at least two surgeries (100 %), compared with 14 of 64 non-recurrent patients (21.9 %), with a median time to second surgery of 629.5 days (IQR, 366.8–1,050.5) versus 116.5 days (IQR, 49.5–241.8) among re-operated non-recurrent patients. Recurrent no/negative patients were enriched for margin involvement (75.0 % vs 18.8 %), RAI receipt (75.0 % vs 15.6 %), and AGES score (median 8.90 [IQR, 7.72–9.98] vs 5.86 [IQR, 3.00–7.15]).

# Discussion

In this strict-DTC surgical cohort of 3,578 patients with 105 path-proven recurrences, gross ETE was independently associated with higher odds of path-proven recurrence than microscopic ETE after adjustment for age, sex, log-transformed tumor size, AJCC eighth-edition N stage, five-level histology, and separate five-level lymphatic and vascular invasion categories. The primary estimate was clinically meaningful and statistically significant (aOR, 1.77; 95 % CI, 1.15–2.71; *p* = 0.009). The association was directionally consistent across sensitivity and exploratory analyses, including molecular adjustment, lymph-node-topography adjustment, PTC-only restriction, composite-LVI modeling, and follow-up-restricted analyses. The sole attenuated nonsignificant specification was the RAI-adjusted model, in which RAI itself entered strongly, consistent with confounding by indication rather than a causal adverse effect of RAI.

These findings provide pathologic support, from the outcomes side, for the AJCC eighth-edition decision to separate microscopic ETE from gross ETE for anatomic staging.¹,² They do not imply that microscopic ETE is clinically irrelevant. Rather, microscopic ETE should be interpreted as one component of individualized recurrence-risk assessment, integrated with tumor size, N stage and nodal topography, number and size of involved lymph nodes, extranodal extension when available, LVI subtype, aggressive histology, margin status, postoperative thyroglobulin, molecular data when complete, and dynamic response to therapy.³,⁹ This interpretation reconciles AJCC eighth-edition de-escalation for mortality staging with American Thyroid Association–style recurrence-risk assessment.

A plausible biologic explanation is that gross ETE represents a more advanced invasion phenotype accompanied by greater primary-tumor burden, lymphatic access, and nodal dissemination. Gross ETE was associated with larger tumors, higher central- (31.3 % vs 16.9 %) and lateral- (14.6 % vs 5.6 %) compartment lymph-node positivity, greater margin involvement, and more LVI than microscopic ETE. The lymph-node-topography exploratory model preserved and slightly sharpened the gross-ETE signal (aOR 1.99) while showing that both central and lateral nodal positivity were independently associated with recurrence; the ETE × nodal-burden interaction was not significant (interaction *p* = 0.485), suggesting an additive risk architecture in which gross invasion, tumor size, vascular invasion, and nodal burden each contribute to observed recurrence.

Separating lymphatic from vascular invasion added information beyond a composite LVI variable. The composite-LVI model preserved the gross-vs-microscopic ETE association, but the primary model revealed a distinct vascular signal: focal vascular invasion was independently associated with recurrence (aOR 2.25; 95 % CI, 1.07–4.73; *p* = 0.033), whereas lymphatic invasion present versus missing was not (aOR 0.87; 95 % CI, 0.47–1.61; *p* = 0.657). Because lymphatic and vascular invasion may reflect different routes of tumor dissemination and are reported with different degrees of completeness, combining them into a single flag risks both biologic oversimplification and statistical misclassification. Future pathology workflows should pursue more complete and standardized reporting of both components.

## The no/negative ETE subgroup as a confounded comparator

The no/negative ETE subgroup warrants careful interpretation. Its crude path-proven recurrence rate appeared paradoxically elevated compared with microscopic ETE (4.4 % vs 1.9 %), and its adjusted no/negative-vs-microscopic OR was point-elevated but imprecise (aOR, 2.72; 95 % CI, 0.80–9.30; *p* = 0.111). The structured audit (Table 4) provides five converging lines of quantitative evidence that this apparent excess is an artifact of ascertainment bias, referral complexity, and surgical-selection effects rather than a biologic signal.

First, the follow-up differential was extreme: the four composite-recurrent no/negative patients had a median follow-up of 9.68 years (IQR, 4.53–13.84), compared with 1.20 years (IQR, 0.00–5.91) among non-recurrent no/negative patients — an approximately 8-fold difference. Second, all four recurrent patients (100 %) underwent at least two surgeries, compared with 21.9 % of non-recurrent patients; the time to second surgery was substantially longer among recurrent patients (median 629.5 days; IQR, 366.8–1,050.5) than among re-operated non-recurrent patients (median 116.5 days; IQR, 49.5–241.8). Third, recurrent no/negative patients were enriched for margin involvement (75 % vs 18.8 %), RAI receipt (75 % vs 15.6 %), and AGES score (median 8.90 [IQR, 7.72–9.98] vs 5.86 [IQR, 3.00–7.15]). Fourth, the no/negative subgroup itself was enriched for atypical referral pathways: 26.5 % had ≥ 2 surgeries (vs 14.9 % in microscopic ETE), and 23.5 % had Nx status (vs 6.1 % in microscopic ETE). Fifth, in a prespecified sensitivity analysis excluding patients with zero recorded follow-up, the gross-vs-microscopic signal was preserved (aOR 1.79; 95 % CI, 1.17–2.72; *p* = 0.007) while the no/negative-vs-microscopic contrast remained non-significant (aOR 2.46; 95 % CI, 0.72–8.42; *p* = 0.150).

We therefore designate the no/negative ETE group a confounded comparator. It is appropriate evidence that the no/negative-vs-microscopic contrast is underpowered and ascertainment-confounded; it is not evidence that absent ETE paradoxically increases recurrence risk. The AJCC eighth-edition framework is designed to distinguish gross from microscopic ETE, not to characterize the small population of patients with formally documented absent ETE — itself a pathologic finding subject to reporting and ascertainment variability.

## Statistical power and model stability

Statistical power was asymmetric across the two principal contrasts. For the gross-vs-microscopic comparison, with 57 versus 45 events in groups of 1,151 and 2,359 patients, the study has greater than 90 % power to detect an OR of 2.0 and approximately 70 % power to detect an OR of 1.5 at α = 0.05. The observed aOR of 1.77 was detected with *p* = 0.009 and was stable across model specifications. For the no/negative-vs-microscopic comparison, with 3 events in 68 patients versus 45 events in 2,359 patients, power to detect an OR of 2.5 at α = 0.05 is less than 30 %.

The events-per-variable (EPV) ratio for the full primary model is 5.8 (105 events across approximately 18 parameters), below the conventional threshold of 10 proposed by Peduzzi and colleagues. This is a recognized limitation, mitigated by four features of the analysis: the primary inference concerns the two-degree-of-freedom ETE contrast rather than the full covariate set; quasi-separated strata (Nx, high-grade DTC, lymphatic-focal) are transparently detected and dropped; sensitivity analyses with reduced covariate sets produce similar ETE estimates; and the direction and significance of the gross-vs-microscopic contrast are stable across all model specifications that do not include RAI.

## CT imaging context

The differential prognostic significance of gross versus microscopic ETE identified in our study is best interpreted alongside the preoperative imaging literature. A focused systematic review of 12 CT studies in surgical thyroid-cancer cohorts using pathology as the reference standard demonstrates a fundamental asymmetry in CT diagnostic performance: CT specificity for ETE is consistently high (85 %–98 %) when structured radiologic criteria are used, whereas sensitivity is variable (14 %–99 %; typically 40 %–78 % with structured criteria). CT performs substantially better for gross ETE (~80 % sensitivity; AUC 0.80) than for microscopic ETE (~50 % sensitivity; AUC 0.72).¹¹,¹² Performance also varied by anatomical structure, with vascular invasion showing higher sensitivity (75 %–83 %) than esophageal invasion (29 %–77 %). Importantly, fewer than one-third of these primary studies acknowledged surgical selection bias, and over half of the meta-analyzed studies were judged to be at high risk of bias.

This imaging-performance profile is coherent with our pathology-based outcomes findings. Gross ETE — the subtype that independently predicts recurrence — is also the subtype that CT can detect with reasonable sensitivity and high specificity. Conversely, microscopic ETE is both less reproducibly detected on CT and less strongly associated with recurrence after adjustment. These complementary findings support a clinical pathway in which preoperative CT is most valuable when gross invasion is suspected and when positive findings may alter surgical planning, whereas postoperative pathology remains essential for definitive ETE classification. Emerging radiomics-based approaches (AUC 0.78–0.84 in preliminary studies) may improve preoperative gross-ETE detection without sacrificing specificity. We did not have structured preoperative CT ETE variables in this dataset, so direct correlation of imaging classification, surgical decision-making, and pathologic ETE grade was not possible — an important data gap that future studies should close.

## Generalizability and the zero-follow-up challenge

One-third of the cohort (1,187 of 3,578; 33.2 %) had zero recorded follow-up years. This data feature has three implications. First, it precludes Cox proportional-hazards modeling. Second, it inflates rate denominators without contributing comparable surveillance time: only one of the 1,187 zero-follow-up patients had a path-proven recurrence. Third, it could introduce selection bias if zero follow-up is non-random across ETE groups — and indeed it is, with 37.9 % of microscopic-ETE patients but only 23.4 % of gross-ETE patients having zero follow-up. We addressed this with prespecified sensitivity analyses described in the Results: excluding zero-follow-up patients yielded aOR 1.79 (*p* = 0.007), and excluding patients with less than one year of follow-up yielded aOR 2.10 (*p* = 0.002), with N1b reaching significance in the latter cohort. The primary findings are therefore not artifacts of zero-follow-up dilution and, if anything, are conservative.

This is a single-institution study from a large academic referral center and may over-represent complex or higher-risk cases relative to community practice. Practice patterns for surgery, pathology reporting, RAI selection, and surveillance may also differ across institutions. Single-institution data offer the advantage of consistent pathologic classification, uniform ETE grading, and a single electronic health record for endpoint ascertainment, all of which reduce measurement error in the exposure and outcome. Multicenter validation with prospective ETE grading and standardized follow-up nevertheless remains an essential next step.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations beyond those discussed above. The retrospective design relies on existing pathology classifications and follow-up workflows. Follow-up was short and uneven, and late recurrences (≥ 5 years) are likely under-ascertained. The binary logistic framework cannot estimate hazard rates, recurrence-free survival, or proportional-hazards assumptions, and the EPV of 5.8 limits precision for sparse categories, interactions, and subgroup analyses. The dataset did not contain structured preoperative CT ETE variables, radiology-defined ETE grade, or CT–pathology concordance fields. BRAF and TERT testing was not universal. Finally, the no/negative ETE subgroup was small, atypical, and underpowered, preventing a reliable propensity-matched comparison with microscopic ETE.

## Future directions

Future work should validate these findings in multicenter cohorts with prospective ETE grading, longer standardized follow-up, detailed nodal-burden annotation including extranodal extension, postoperative thyroglobulin, and dynamic response to therapy. Imaging integration should include structured CT reporting, ultrasound–CT fusion variables, radiomics, and machine-learning approaches designed to improve preoperative detection of gross ETE while preserving the high specificity needed for surgical planning. Expanding the no/negative ETE subgroup through multicenter collaboration (target n ≥ 200 with ≥ 10 events) would enable a propensity-matched comparison with microscopic ETE.

# Conclusion

In a strict-DTC surgical cohort with path-proven recurrence as the primary endpoint, gross ETE was associated with higher adjusted odds of recurrence than microscopic ETE. The small no/negative ETE subgroup was underpowered and confounded by follow-up, reoperation, margin, RAI, and referral-selection effects. These findings support the AJCC eighth-edition separation of microscopic from gross ETE for anatomic staging while preserving microscopic ETE as a contextual feature in individualized recurrence-risk assessment.
