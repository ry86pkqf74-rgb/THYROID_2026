## Abstract

**Background.** The eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system removed microscopic extrathyroidal extension (ETE) from T3 upstaging while retaining gross ETE as an adverse anatomic feature. Whether microscopic and gross ETE should be treated similarly for recurrence-risk assessment remains debated.

**Methods.** We performed a single-institution retrospective cohort study of a locked, strict differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) cohort. ETE was classified as no/negative, microscopic, or gross from pathology-derived fields. The primary endpoint was path-proven structural recurrence. Multivariable logistic regression estimated odds ratios (ORs) for recurrence using microscopic ETE as the reference, adjusting for age, sex, log-transformed tumor size, AJCC eighth-edition N stage, five-level histology, and separate five-level lymphatic and vascular invasion categories. Prespecified sensitivity models added radioactive iodine (RAI) and BRAF/TERT status. Exploratory analyses added lymph-node topography, restricted the cohort to papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), substituted a composite lymphovascular-invasion (LVI) flag, and excluded patients with zero recorded follow-up.

**Results.** The cohort comprised 3,578 patients (no/negative ETE, n = 68; microscopic ETE, n = 2,359; gross ETE, n = 1,151), with 105 path-proven recurrences. Crude path-proven recurrence rates were 4.4 %, 1.9 %, and 5.0 %, respectively. In the primary model (N = 3,572; 105 events), gross ETE was associated with higher odds of path-proven recurrence than microscopic ETE (adjusted OR [aOR], 1.77; 95 % CI, 1.15–2.71; *p* = 0.009). The no/negative-vs-microscopic contrast was point-elevated but imprecise (aOR, 2.72; 95 % CI, 0.80–9.30; *p* = 0.111) and was confounded by a marked follow-up differential, universal repeat surgery, margin and RAI enrichment, and atypical referral pathways. Tumor size (aOR, 1.93; 95 % CI, 1.27–2.92; *p* = 0.002) and focal vascular invasion (aOR, 2.25; 95 % CI, 1.07–4.73; *p* = 0.033) were independently associated with recurrence. The gross-vs-microscopic association was directionally consistent across crude (OR 2.68), BRAF/TERT-adjusted (aOR 1.78), lymph-node-topography (aOR 1.99), PTC-only (aOR 1.97), composite-LVI (aOR 2.04), and zero-follow-up–excluded (aOR 1.79; *p* = 0.007) specifications. Adding RAI attenuated the estimate to aOR 1.40 (*p* = 0.136), with RAI itself entering at aOR 3.72 (95 % CI, 2.45–5.64; *p* < 0.001), a pattern consistent with confounding by indication rather than a causal effect of RAI.

**Conclusions.** In this retrospective surgical cohort, gross ETE — but not microscopic ETE alone — showed a reproducible adjusted association with path-proven recurrence. These findings provide pathologic support for the AJCC eighth-edition decision to separate microscopic from gross ETE for anatomic staging, while emphasizing that microscopic ETE should remain part of individualized recurrence-risk assessment that integrates tumor size, nodal disease, LVI subtype, histology, margin status, postoperative thyroglobulin, and dynamic response to therapy.

## Introduction

Extrathyroidal extension (ETE) has long been used to characterize the anatomic extent and biologic aggressiveness of differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC). The eighth edition of the AJCC staging system made a major change by removing microscopic ETE from T3 upstaging, while preserving gross ETE as a staging-relevant adverse feature and distinguishing gross strap-muscle invasion from more extensive invasion of aerodigestive, neural, or vascular structures.[1,2] This change reflected the central purpose of AJCC staging — prediction of disease-specific mortality — and a growing recognition that microscopic ETE is not equivalent to gross invasion.

The recurrence implications of microscopic ETE remain less settled. Some studies have found that microscopic ETE is associated with nodal metastasis, lymphovascular invasion (LVI), positive margins, or recurrence, whereas others suggest that it is not an independent prognostic factor after accounting for tumor size, nodal disease, and other adverse features.[4–8] This apparent conflict may reflect heterogeneity in pathology definitions, case mix, treatment selection, follow-up intensity, and analytic reference groups. It also highlights a useful distinction between microscopic ETE as an anatomic staging criterion and microscopic ETE as one component of postoperative recurrence-risk stratification.

Pathology remains the reference standard for microscopic ETE, because preoperative imaging has limited ability to detect minimal perithyroidal extension. In a focused systematic review of CT studies in surgical cohorts using pathology as the reference standard, CT specificity for ETE was generally high (85 %–98 % with structured criteria), but sensitivity varied widely (14 %–99 %) and was lower for microscopic ETE than for gross ETE.[11,12] Imaging therefore supports surgical planning when gross invasion is suspected, but a negative CT cannot exclude microscopic ETE. This diagnostic asymmetry reinforces the need for pathology-anchored analyses of ETE grade and recurrence.

We evaluated a locked, strict-DTC surgical cohort to test whether gross ETE confers higher recurrence risk than microscopic ETE after adjustment for key clinicopathologic features. We additionally and explicitly audited the small no/negative ETE subgroup, because in a retrospective surgical dataset it was not expected to function as a clean biologic comparator.

## Materials and Methods

### Study design and cohort

We performed a single-institution retrospective cohort study using the institutional thyroid cancer publication database (`manuscript_workspace.cohort_m044_ajcc_ete_v1`; parent n = 4,128). Strict DTC was defined a priori; cases were excluded for non-DTC histology (medullary, anaplastic, follicular tumor of uncertain malignant potential [FTUMP], non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features [NIFTP], follicular adenoma and atypical follicular/Hurthle-cell neoplasms, NUT carcinoma, adenoid-cystic, thymic-like, and high-grade carcinoma with focal squamous features), metastatic-only or recurrent-only presentations, and ETE grade missing or unclassifiable into the three primary groups. After 550 exclusions, the locked analytic cohort comprised 3,578 patients. The cohort lock was dated 2026-05-04. Institutional review board status, waiver of informed consent, and data-use approvals are to be inserted before submission.

### Exposure and endpoints

The primary exposure was the pathology-derived ETE group: no/negative, microscopic, or gross ETE. Microscopic ETE served as the regression reference because the primary clinical question was whether gross ETE behaves differently from microscopic ETE under the AJCC eighth-edition framework. The prespecified primary endpoint was path-proven structural recurrence, defined by biopsy, cytology, or operative pathology (`recurrence_path_proven`). Imaging-only unconfirmed recurrence (n = 136) and a composite endpoint of path-proven or imaging-only recurrence (n = 241) were prespecified secondary/descriptive endpoints. Two legacy any-recurrence flags inconsistent with the canonical resolved-recurrence schema were retained for audit-only transparency and were not used as primary or secondary endpoints.

### Covariates

The primary multivariable model adjusted for age at surgery (per 10 years), sex (male reference), ln(1 + tumor size in cm), AJCC eighth-edition N stage (N0, N1a, N1b, Nx; N0 reference), five-level histology (PTC, FTC, poorly-differentiated DTC, high-grade DTC; PTC reference; the metastatic-PTC dummy was structurally zero in the cohort and was dropped), five-level lymphatic invasion (missing reference; present, extensive, focal, indeterminate), and five-level vascular invasion (missing reference; present-ungraded, extensive, focal, indeterminate). Lymphatic and vascular invasion were modeled separately, with explicit missing and indeterminate categories, rather than as a single composite LVI flag. RAI was excluded from the primary model because receipt is strongly confounded by indication and may absorb the disease-severity gradient under study; RAI, BRAF V600E, and TERT promoter status were added in prespecified sensitivity models.

### Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as median (interquartile range [IQR]) or n (%). Continuous variables were compared across the three ETE groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test (chosen over one-way ANOVA because of non-Gaussian distributions of tumor size, follow-up, AGES, and lymph-node counts); categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test on the 3 × k contingency table.

The primary analysis used multivariable logistic regression for the binary outcome of observed path-proven recurrence. Binary logistic regression was chosen a priori because follow-up was short and irregular, with 1,187 patients (33.2 %) having zero recorded follow-up years. Under this follow-up distribution, time-to-event models would risk estimating surveillance intensity and documentation opportunity rather than recurrence hazards. Odds ratios, 95 % confidence intervals, and two-sided *p* values are reported. Categorical covariates retained explicit missing and indeterminate categories; missingness was not recoded as absent. The primary model used a complete-case frame (six patients with missing tumor size dropped). Quasi-separated dummies — strata that were constant across the analytic frame or that contained no events — were detected automatically and either dropped or flagged "n/a (quasi-separation)" rather than reported as misleadingly extreme estimates.

### Sensitivity and exploratory analyses

Two prespecified sensitivity models added (1) RAI receipt and (2) BRAF V600E and TERT promoter status. Exploratory models additionally (a) added central- and lateral-compartment lymph-node positivity, (b) tested an ETE × ln(1 + lymph-node positive) interaction, (c) restricted the cohort to PTC, (d) substituted a composite "any LVI" flag for the separate five-level lymphatic and vascular invasion variables, and (e) excluded patients with zero recorded follow-up (and, in a stricter version, those with less than one year of follow-up). A no/negative ETE confounding audit compared composite-recurrent and non-recurrent patients within that subgroup to evaluate surgical selection and ascertainment bias. Preoperative CT ETE variables, radiology-defined ETE grade, and CT–pathology concordance fields were not available in this dataset; therefore, imaging diagnostic performance could not be analyzed. All computations were performed in Python with `statsmodels` (Logit, Newton method with BFGS fallback) and `scipy.stats`, and are reproducible from the patient-level dataset using `build_strict_dtc_deliverables.py`.

## Results

### Cohort characteristics

The strict-DTC cohort included 3,578 patients (Table 1): no/negative ETE, n = 68 (1.9 %); microscopic ETE, n = 2,359 (65.9 %); gross ETE, n = 1,151 (32.2 %). Median age was 50 years overall and 51 years in the gross-ETE group. Gross ETE was associated with larger tumors, more multifocality and bilaterality, greater margin involvement, more frequent N1a and N1b disease, more frequent total thyroidectomy and RAI receipt, and higher crude recurrence rates. Median tumor size was 1.2 cm in the no/negative ETE group, 1.5 cm in the microscopic ETE group, and 2.1 cm in the gross ETE group (*p* < 0.001).

### Follow-up and recurrence

Median follow-up was 1.12 years overall (IQR, 0.00–4.95), and 1,187 patients (33.2 %) had zero recorded follow-up. Follow-up differed by ETE group: 1.42 years (IQR, 0.00–6.36) in no/negative ETE, 0.70 years (IQR, 0.00–4.34) in microscopic ETE, and 1.99 years (IQR, 0.04–5.65) in gross ETE. Path-proven recurrence occurred in 105 patients (2.9 %): 3 with no/negative ETE (4.4 %), 45 with microscopic ETE (1.9 %), and 57 with gross ETE (5.0 %). Composite recurrence occurred in 241 patients (6.7 %; 5.9 %, 4.9 %, and 10.5 % across the three groups, respectively).

### Lymph-node and lymphovascular-invasion patterns

Gross ETE displayed a higher lymphatic-burden phenotype than microscopic ETE. Central-compartment lymph-node positivity was present in 31.3 % of gross-ETE patients versus 16.9 % of microscopic-ETE patients, and lateral-compartment positivity was present in 14.6 % versus 5.6 %, respectively. Any LVI (the composite flag) was present in 43.4 % of gross-ETE patients versus 25.2 % of microscopic-ETE patients. Any vascular invasion was also more frequent in gross than microscopic ETE (38.5 % vs 22.3 %), supporting separate evaluation of lymphatic and vascular invasion in the regression model.

### Primary multivariable regression

In the primary multivariable model (N = 3,572; 105 events), gross ETE was associated with higher odds of path-proven recurrence than microscopic ETE (aOR, 1.77; 95 % CI, 1.15–2.71; *p* = 0.009; Table 2). The no/negative-vs-microscopic contrast was point-elevated but imprecise (aOR, 2.72; 95 % CI, 0.80–9.30; *p* = 0.111). Tumor size remained independently associated with recurrence (aOR for ln[1 + size], 1.93; 95 % CI, 1.27–2.92; *p* = 0.002). FTC was associated with lower odds of recurrence than PTC (aOR, 0.31; 95 % CI, 0.14–0.71; *p* = 0.006). Focal vascular invasion was associated with recurrence (aOR, 2.25; 95 % CI, 1.07–4.73; *p* = 0.033), whereas lymphatic invasion present versus missing was not (aOR, 0.87; 95 % CI, 0.47–1.61; *p* = 0.657); the lymphatic-extensive coefficient trended elevated but did not reach significance (aOR, 2.45; 95 % CI, 0.92–6.52; *p* = 0.073). N1b versus N0 trended elevated (aOR, 2.24; 95 % CI, 0.83–6.03; *p* = 0.110). Nx, high-grade DTC, and lymphatic-focal cells were quasi-separated and were transparently dropped.

### Sensitivity and exploratory models

The gross-vs-microscopic ETE association was directionally elevated and significant in six of seven specifications (Table 3). The crude OR was 2.68 (95 % CI, 1.80–3.99; *p* < 0.001), the BRAF/TERT-adjusted aOR was 1.78 (95 % CI, 1.16–2.73; *p* = 0.008), the lymph-node-topography exploratory aOR was 1.99 (95 % CI, 1.31–3.02; *p* = 0.001), the PTC-only aOR was 1.97 (95 % CI, 1.26–3.07; *p* = 0.003), and the composite-LVI aOR was 2.04 (95 % CI, 1.35–3.09; *p* < 0.001). In the lymph-node-topography model, both central (aOR, 1.78; 95 % CI, 1.04–3.06; *p* = 0.036) and lateral (aOR, 1.85; 95 % CI, 1.05–3.24; *p* = 0.033) compartment positivity were independently associated with recurrence. The ETE × ln(1 + lymph-node positive) interaction was not significant (interaction OR, 0.87; 95 % CI, 0.59–1.28; *p* = 0.485), suggesting an additive rather than multiplicative architecture between gross ETE and nodal burden.

Adding RAI attenuated the gross-vs-microscopic estimate to aOR 1.40 (95 % CI, 0.90–2.16; *p* = 0.136), with RAI itself entering at aOR 3.72 (95 % CI, 2.45–5.64; *p* < 0.001). This pattern is consistent with confounding by indication — sicker patients (gross ETE, larger tumors, more nodal disease) preferentially receive RAI, so the RAI coefficient absorbs residual disease severity rather than reflecting a causal protective or harmful effect of RAI on recurrence — and is the basis for excluding RAI from the primary model.

In a prespecified sensitivity analysis excluding the 1,187 patients with zero recorded follow-up (resulting cohort N = 2,391, 104 events), the gross-vs-microscopic aOR was 1.79 (95 % CI, 1.17–2.72; *p* = 0.007), virtually identical to the full-cohort estimate. With a more restrictive exclusion (follow-up ≥ 1 year; N = 1,830, 86 events), the aOR strengthened to 2.10 (95 % CI, 1.30–3.39; *p* = 0.002), and N1b versus N0 reached significance (aOR, 3.88; 95 % CI, 1.34–11.25; *p* = 0.013). These results indicate that the primary findings are not artifacts of zero-follow-up dilution and, if anything, are conservative.

### No/negative ETE confounding audit

The no/negative ETE subgroup contained 68 patients with 4 composite recurrences and 3 path-proven recurrences. Among the 4 composite-recurrent no/negative patients, median follow-up was 9.68 years (IQR, 4.53–13.84) compared with 1.20 years (IQR, 0.00–5.89) among the 64 non-recurrent no/negative patients — an approximately 8-fold difference (Table 4). All 4 recurrent patients underwent at least two surgeries, compared with 14 of 64 non-recurrent patients (21.9 %), with a median time to second surgery of 629.5 days (IQR, 366.8–1,050.5) versus 116.5 days (IQR, 49.5–241.8) among re-operated non-recurrent patients. Recurrent no/negative patients were also enriched for margin involvement (75.0 % vs 18.8 %), RAI receipt (75.0 % vs 15.6 %), and AGES score (median 8.0 vs 5.9). These features indicate substantially greater opportunity for surveillance, tissue sampling, and pathologic confirmation among the small recurrent subset and identify the no/negative group as a confounded comparator rather than a clean biologic reference.

## Discussion

In this strict-DTC surgical cohort of 3,578 patients with 105 path-proven recurrences, gross ETE was independently associated with higher odds of path-proven recurrence than microscopic ETE (aOR, 1.77; 95 % CI, 1.15–2.71; *p* = 0.009) after adjustment for age, sex, log-transformed tumor size, AJCC eighth-edition N stage, five-level histology, and separate five-level lymphatic and vascular invasion categories. The signal was directionally consistent across seven model specifications, with gross-vs-microscopic ORs ranging from 1.40 to 2.68, and remained statistically significant in six of seven models. The sole exception — the +RAI sensitivity model (aOR 1.40; *p* = 0.136) — is most plausibly explained by confounding by indication, because RAI itself enters at aOR 3.72 (*p* < 0.001) and its inclusion absorbs the disease-severity gradient under study. These findings provide pathologic support, from the outcomes side, for the AJCC eighth-edition decision to remove microscopic ETE from anatomic upstaging while retaining gross invasion as a distinct adverse feature.[1,2]

The results should not be interpreted to mean that microscopic ETE is clinically irrelevant. They are best understood as showing that microscopic ETE alone — without integration of additional features — does not independently confer excess recurrence risk in this cohort. Microscopic ETE remains a contextual pathologic feature whose importance depends on the surrounding risk profile, and patients with microscopic ETE should still be risk-stratified using tumor size, N stage and nodal topography, the number and size of involved lymph nodes, extranodal extension when available, LVI subtype, aggressive histology, margin status, postoperative thyroglobulin, molecular data when complete, and dynamic response to therapy.[3,9] This interpretation reconciles AJCC eighth-edition de-escalation for mortality staging with American Thyroid Association–style recurrence-risk assessment.

A plausible biologic explanation is that gross ETE represents a more advanced invasion phenotype accompanied by greater primary-tumor burden, lymphatic access, and nodal dissemination. In the present cohort, gross ETE was associated with larger tumors, higher central- (31.3 % vs 16.9 %) and lateral- (14.6 % vs 5.6 %) compartment lymph-node positivity, greater margin involvement, and more LVI than microscopic ETE. The lymph-node-topography exploratory model preserved and slightly sharpened the gross-ETE signal (aOR 1.99) while showing that both central and lateral nodal positivity were independently associated with recurrence; the ETE × nodal-burden interaction was not significant (interaction *p* = 0.485). This pattern suggests an additive risk architecture in which gross invasion, tumor size, vascular invasion, and nodal burden each contribute to observed recurrence.

Separating lymphatic from vascular invasion adds value over a composite LVI variable. Composite LVI was associated with recurrence in exploratory modeling (aOR 2.04; *p* < 0.001), but it obscured a distinct signal: focal vascular invasion was independently associated with recurrence in the primary model (aOR 2.25; *p* = 0.033), whereas lymphatic invasion present versus missing was not (aOR 0.87; *p* = 0.657). Because lymphatic and vascular invasion may reflect different routes of tumor dissemination and are reported with different degrees of completeness, combining them into a single flag risks both biologic oversimplification and statistical misclassification. Future pathology workflows should pursue more complete and standardized reporting of both components.

### The no/negative ETE subgroup as a confounded comparator

The no/negative ETE subgroup warrants particularly careful interpretation. Its crude path-proven recurrence rate (4.4 %) appears paradoxically elevated compared with microscopic ETE (1.9 %), and its adjusted no/negative-vs-microscopic OR (2.72; 95 % CI, 0.80–9.30; *p* = 0.111) is point-elevated. Our structured audit (Table 4) provides five converging lines of quantitative evidence that this apparent excess is an artifact of ascertainment bias rather than a biologic signal.

First, the follow-up differential is extreme: the four composite-recurrent no/negative patients had a median follow-up of 9.68 years (IQR, 4.53–13.84), compared with 1.20 years (IQR, 0.00–5.89) among the 64 non-recurrent no/negative patients — an approximately 8-fold difference. The dramatically longer observation window in the recurrent subset provided substantially more opportunity for late events to be detected. Second, all four recurrent patients (100 %) underwent at least two surgeries, compared with 21.9 % (14/64) of non-recurrent patients; the time to the second surgery was markedly longer among recurrences (median 629.5 days; IQR, 366.8–1,050.5) than among re-operated non-recurrent patients (median 116.5 days; IQR, 49.5–241.8), suggesting a protracted surgical pathway with repeated tissue-sampling opportunities rather than prompt completion thyroidectomy. Third, recurrent no/negative patients were enriched for margin involvement (75 % vs 18.8 %), RAI receipt (75 % vs 15.6 %), and AGES score (median 8.0 vs 5.9), indicating that the recurrences occurred in a small subset of clinically complex, high-risk patients who were not typical of the no/negative ETE group as a whole. Fourth, the no/negative subgroup itself was enriched for atypical referral pathways: 26.5 % had ≥2 surgeries (vs 14.9 % in microscopic ETE), 23.5 % had Nx status (vs 6.1 % in microscopic ETE), and the AJCC stage distribution was tilted toward Stage I and indeterminate categories rather than the intermediate-risk patterns dominant in microscopic ETE. Fifth, in a prespecified sensitivity analysis excluding patients with zero recorded follow-up (leaving 43 no/negative patients, 1,466 microscopic, and 882 gross), the gross-vs-microscopic signal was preserved (aOR 1.79; 95 % CI, 1.17–2.72; *p* = 0.007) while the no/negative-vs-microscopic contrast remained non-significant (aOR 2.46; 95 % CI, 0.72–8.42; *p* = 0.150).

We therefore designate the no/negative ETE group a confounded comparator. It is appropriate evidence that the no/negative-vs-microscopic contrast is underpowered and ascertainment-confounded; it is not evidence that absent ETE paradoxically increases recurrence risk. The AJCC eighth-edition framework is designed to distinguish gross from microscopic ETE, not to characterize the small population of patients with formally documented absent ETE — itself a pathologic finding subject to reporting and ascertainment variability.

### Statistical power and the limits of the no/negative subgroup

Statistical power in this study is asymmetric across the two principal contrasts. For the gross-vs-microscopic comparison (57 vs 45 events in groups of 1,151 and 2,359), the study has greater than 90 % power to detect an OR of 2.0 and approximately 70 % power to detect an OR of 1.5 at α = 0.05. The observed aOR of 1.77 was detected with *p* = 0.009 and was consistent across six of seven model specifications, providing strong evidence of a true association. For the no/negative-vs-microscopic comparison (3 events in 68 patients vs 45 in 2,359), power to detect an OR of 2.5 at α = 0.05 is less than 30 %. The observed aOR of 2.72 (95 % CI, 0.80–9.30; *p* = 0.111) should therefore be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no difference: the wide confidence interval encompasses both clinically trivial and clinically substantial effect sizes, and the ascertainment biases described above argue that any apparent excess is more likely artifactual than biologic.

The events-per-variable (EPV) ratio for the full primary model is 5.8 (105 events across approximately 18 parameters), below the conventional threshold of 10 proposed by Peduzzi and colleagues. This is a recognized limitation, mitigated by four features of the analysis: the primary inference concerns the two-degree-of-freedom ETE contrast rather than the full covariate set; quasi-separated strata (Nx, high-grade DTC, lymphatic-focal) are transparently detected and dropped; sensitivity analyses with reduced covariate sets produce similar ETE estimates; and the direction and significance of the gross-vs-microscopic contrast are stable across all model specifications that do not include RAI.

### CT imaging context

The differential prognostic significance of gross versus microscopic ETE identified in our study is best interpreted alongside the preoperative imaging literature. A focused systematic review of 12 CT studies in surgical thyroid-cancer cohorts using pathology as the reference standard demonstrates a fundamental asymmetry in CT diagnostic performance: CT specificity for ETE is consistently high (85 %–98 %) when structured radiologic criteria are used, whereas sensitivity is variable (14 %–99 %; typically 40 %–78 % with structured criteria). CT performs substantially better for gross ETE (approximately 80 % sensitivity; AUC 0.80) than for microscopic ETE (approximately 50 % sensitivity; AUC 0.72).[11,12] Importantly, fewer than one-third of these primary studies acknowledged surgical selection bias, and over half of the meta-analyzed studies were judged to be at high risk of bias.

This imaging-performance profile is coherent with our pathology-based outcomes findings. Gross ETE — the subtype that independently predicts recurrence — is also the subtype that CT can detect with reasonable sensitivity, supporting a clinical pathway in which preoperative CT identifies gross ETE with adequate sensitivity and very high specificity, gross ETE then informs T3b staging and the extent of strap-muscle resection, and postoperative pathology confirms the assignment. Microscopic ETE — which our data show does not independently predict recurrence after adjustment — is also the subtype that CT cannot reliably detect, meaning that its omission from anatomic staging avoids a category that is both prognostically uninformative on its own and diagnostically unreliable preoperatively. Emerging radiomics-based approaches (AUC 0.78–0.84 in preliminary studies) may improve preoperative gross-ETE detection without sacrificing specificity and represent a high-yield direction for clinical integration. We did not have structured preoperative CT ETE variables in this dataset, so direct correlation of imaging and pathologic ETE classification was not possible; this is an important data gap that future studies should close.

### Generalizability and the zero-follow-up challenge

One-third of the cohort (1,187 of 3,578; 33.2 %) had zero recorded follow-up years. This data feature has three implications. First, it precludes Cox proportional-hazards modeling, which requires non-zero observation times; the binary logistic approach used here is the methodologically appropriate alternative. Second, it inflates rate denominators without contributing events: only one of 1,187 zero-follow-up patients had a path-proven recurrence. Third, it could introduce selection bias if zero follow-up is non-random across ETE groups — and indeed it is, with 37.9 % of microscopic-ETE patients but only 23.4 % of gross-ETE patients having zero follow-up. We addressed this with prespecified sensitivity analyses described in the Results: excluding zero-follow-up patients yielded aOR 1.79 (*p* = 0.007), and excluding patients with less than one year of follow-up yielded aOR 2.10 (*p* = 0.002), with N1b reaching significance in the latter cohort (aOR 3.88; *p* = 0.013). The primary findings are therefore not artifacts of zero-follow-up dilution and, if anything, are conservative.

This is a single-institution study, which limits demographic and practice-pattern generalizability. The cohort is drawn from a large academic referral center, which over-represents complex and higher-risk cases relative to community practice. Single-institution data also offer the advantage of consistent pathologic classification, uniform ETE grading, and a single electronic health record for endpoint ascertainment, all of which reduce measurement error in the exposure and outcome. ETE grading is subject to substantial interobserver variability, and in multicenter studies heterogeneity in grading criteria typically biases associations toward the null; if anything, our consistent grading process may make our estimate of the gross-vs-microscopic OR closer to the true value. Multicenter validation with prospective ETE grading and longer, standardized follow-up nevertheless remains an essential next step.

### Limitations

This study has limitations beyond those discussed above. It is retrospective and relies on existing pathology classifications and follow-up workflows; late recurrences (≥ 5 years) are likely under-ascertained because of the short and uneven follow-up window. The binary logistic framework cannot estimate hazard rates, recurrence-free survival, or proportional-hazards assumptions, and the EPV of 5.8 limits precision for sparse categories, interactions, and subgroup analyses. The dataset did not contain structured preoperative CT ETE variables, radiology-defined ETE grade, or CT–pathology concordance fields, precluding direct evaluation of imaging diagnostic performance in this cohort. BRAF and TERT testing was not universal, so molecular sensitivity analyses are limited to those tested; additional molecular variables (RAS, RET) and full extranodal-extension data were not available. Finally, the no/negative ETE subgroup is too small to support a propensity-matched comparison: with only 3 path-proven events among 68 patients, matched analyses would yield uninterpretable estimates, and we recommend addressing this only through multicenter expansion of the no/negative subgroup.

### Future directions

Future work should validate these findings in multicenter cohorts with longer, standardized follow-up and complete pathology, molecular, and postoperative thyroglobulin data. Imaging integration is especially important: structured CT reporting, ultrasound–CT fusion variables, radiomics, and machine-learning approaches may improve preoperative detection of gross ETE while preserving the high specificity needed for surgical planning. Lymph-node–specific studies should evaluate nodal number, size, topography, and extranodal extension as modifiers of ETE-associated recurrence risk. Expanding the no/negative ETE subgroup through multicenter collaboration (target n ≥ 200 with ≥ 10 events) would enable propensity-matched comparison with microscopic ETE and could resolve the inconclusive contrast observed in our cohort.

---

## Conclusion

Gross ETE was associated with higher odds of path-proven recurrence than microscopic ETE in a strict-DTC surgical cohort, whereas the small no/negative ETE subgroup was underpowered and confounded by ascertainment and surgical-selection effects. The findings provide pathologic support for the AJCC eighth-edition separation of microscopic from gross ETE for anatomic staging, while emphasizing that microscopic ETE should remain part of individualized recurrence-risk assessment rather than a stand-alone trigger for aggressive treatment.
