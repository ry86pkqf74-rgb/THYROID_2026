# RAS-mutated thyroid nodules and surgery: what we learned from 292 patients

A plain-language summary of the M098 study

---

## What this study looked at

When a person has a lump in their thyroid (a "thyroid nodule"), doctors sometimes use a molecular test on a small tissue sample to look for genetic changes that hint at whether the lump might be cancer. One family of these genetic changes is called RAS — the three close cousins are NRAS, HRAS, and KRAS. RAS mutations show up fairly often in thyroid nodules and they sit in a tricky middle zone: many nodules with a RAS change are benign, some turn out to be cancer, and a few are an in-between diagnosis called NIFTP (a tumor that looks like cancer under the microscope but does not behave like cancer).

This study looked at 292 patients who went on to have thyroid surgery after a molecular test found a RAS change in their nodule. We wanted to learn four things:

1. After surgery, how many of these patients turned out to have cancer, an in-between diagnosis, or a benign tumor?
2. Did it matter which specific RAS gene was involved (NRAS vs HRAS vs KRAS)?
3. Did it matter whether the RAS change appeared alone or was joined by another worrying genetic change called a TERT mutation?
4. How does the new 2025 thyroid cancer risk-stratification system compare to the older 2015 system in this group of patients?

## Who was studied

The 292 patients were treated at a single hospital and were de-identified for the study. About 8 in 10 were women, and the median age was 52 years. The group was racially diverse: about 47 in 100 patients identified as White, 39 in 100 as Black or African American, 6 in 100 as Asian, and the rest reported other backgrounds or did not specify. About half had molecular testing through ThyroSeq, a small number through Afirma, and some through both.

## What we found

### 1. About 6 in 10 patients had cancer; about 1 in 14 had an in-between diagnosis

After surgery, 176 of the 292 patients (about 60 in 100) had a final diagnosis of thyroid cancer. Most cancers were the two follicular-pattern types: papillary thyroid carcinoma (the most common) and follicular thyroid carcinoma. A smaller group (22 patients, about 7 in 100) had an in-between diagnosis called NIFTP or FTUMP — these are tumors that look unusual under the microscope but behave gently and do not need aggressive treatment. The remaining 94 patients (about 32 in 100) had benign tumors.

### 2. The specific RAS gene matters a little

Among the patients who had only one type of RAS change:
- NRAS: about 63 in 100 had cancer
- HRAS: about 61 in 100 had cancer
- KRAS: about 43 in 100 had cancer

KRAS-only patients had the lowest cancer rate in this study, but the KRAS group was small (only 14 patients), so this finding needs to be confirmed in larger studies before doctors can use it confidently in decision-making.

### 3. When RAS appears together with TERT, the picture changes

A TERT change is another genetic alteration that, when it appears alongside RAS, signals a more aggressive tumor. Most patients in this study had RAS alone (273 of 292, about 93 in 100), but 14 patients had RAS plus TERT, 4 had RAS plus another gene called BRAF, and 1 patient had all three. The patients with RAS+TERT were the ones who landed most often in the highest-risk category under the new 2025 risk score. The link between TERT and the highest-risk category was strong enough to remain a real finding even after careful statistical adjustment for multiple comparisons.

### 4. The new 2025 risk score moves many patients

Doctors use a "risk score" after thyroid cancer surgery to decide how closely to follow the patient and whether to use radioactive-iodine treatment. The 2015 risk score had three levels (Low / Intermediate / High); the 2025 score has four (Low / Low-Intermediate / Intermediate-High / High).

Of the 181 patients in this cohort who could be scored by the rules:
- 79 patients (about 44 in 100) moved to a higher risk band under the new 2025 system
- 64 patients (about 35 in 100) stayed in the same band
- 38 patients (about 21 in 100) moved to a lower risk band

The biggest movement happened among patients whose 2015 score was "uncalculable" (because some required input was missing). Most of those 85 patients now get a clear assignment under the 2025 system.

### 5. The choice of surgery was driven by classic factors, not by molecular detail alone

Of the 292 patients, 149 had a total thyroidectomy (the whole thyroid removed) and 142 had a lobectomy (half the thyroid removed). The choice between these two options was driven mostly by tumor size and the patient's age, not by whether the RAS change had TERT alongside it. Tumor size was the only feature that independently predicted whether lymph nodes near the thyroid were involved.

## What this means for patients

A few practical takeaways:

A RAS mutation found on a molecular test before surgery does not automatically mean cancer. About 4 in 10 patients in this study turned out to have a benign or in-between diagnosis after surgery.

The company a RAS change keeps matters more than the RAS change by itself. If a TERT change is also present, the risk of more aggressive cancer is higher, and follow-up should be more intensive.

The new 2025 thyroid cancer risk score is more granular than the 2015 score. It will, on average, place more RAS-positive patients into a "mid-tier" risk band rather than leaving them in the previous "uncalculable" gap. Some patients will move up, some will move down — the overall effect is a more individualized assessment.

The lobectomy-versus-total-thyroidectomy decision remains individualized. Tumor size, patient age, and classical pathologic features (like vascular invasion or lymph-node involvement) are the variables that drive that choice, with the molecular result helping to refine — but not replace — those classical signals.

## What the study could not answer

We did not have enough long-term follow-up on most patients to definitively measure how often the cancers in this cohort come back over many years. To preserve the integrity of any recurrence analysis, we focused that part of the work on the subset of patients with at least one full year of follow-up (103 patients). Larger collaborative studies, with longer follow-up, will be needed to confirm the per-gene gradient (especially the low-cancer KRAS observation) and to lock in the 2025 ATA risk score against long-term recurrence outcomes.

## Plain glossary

**RAS mutation** — a change in one of three closely related genes (NRAS, HRAS, KRAS) that controls cell growth signals. RAS changes are common in benign and malignant thyroid nodules alike.

**TERT mutation** — a change in a gene that controls how many times a cell can divide. When TERT changes appear together with RAS, thyroid cancers tend to behave more aggressively.

**ATA risk score** — a system from the American Thyroid Association that estimates how likely a thyroid cancer is to come back after surgery. The 2015 version has three levels; the 2025 version has four.

**NIFTP** — "noninvasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features." A tumor that looks like cancer under the microscope but behaves gently and is treated less aggressively.

**FNA** — fine-needle aspiration. A thin needle is used to take a small sample from a thyroid nodule for analysis before surgery is considered.

**Lobectomy** — surgery to remove half of the thyroid gland. Often the first operation when the diagnosis is uncertain.

**Total thyroidectomy** — surgery to remove the entire thyroid gland. Generally used when cancer is confirmed or strongly suspected.

**ThyroSeq, Afirma** — two commercial molecular testing platforms used on thyroid nodule biopsy samples to look for genetic changes that help refine cancer risk.
